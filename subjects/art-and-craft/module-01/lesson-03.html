Post-Mauryan and Gupta Art

ਪਾਠ 3: ਮੌਰੀਆ ਤੋਂ ਬਾਅਦ ਅਤੇ ਗੁਪਤ ਕਾਲ ਦੀ ਕਲਾ

Why this matters (ERB 2022)

Gupta art questions often focus on the 'Golden Age' characteristics: the shift to structural temples (like Dashavatara Temple, Deogarh) and the ultimate refinement of the Mathura and Sarnath Buddha images.

ਗੁਪਤ ਕਾਲ ਨੂੰ ਭਾਰਤੀ ਕਲਾ ਦਾ 'ਸੁਨਹਿਰੀ ਯੁੱਗ' (Golden Age) ਕਿਹਾ ਜਾਂਦਾ ਹੈ। ਪੇਪਰਾਂ ਵਿੱਚ ਅਕਸਰ ਦੇਵਗੜ੍ਹ ਦੇ ਦਸ਼ਾਵਤਾਰ ਮੰਦਰ ਅਤੇ ਸਾਰਨਾਥ ਦੇ ਬੁੱਧ ਬਾਰੇ ਪੁੱਛਿਆ ਜਾਂਦਾ ਹੈ, ਜਿੱਥੇ ਕਲਾ ਆਪਣੇ ਸਿਖਰ 'ਤੇ ਸੀ।

By the end of this lesson, you will:

  • Identify the key characteristics of the Gupta 'Golden Age' in Indian art.
  • Understand the transition from rock-cut architecture to early structural Hindu temples.
  • Analyze the iconography of the Dashavatara Temple at Deogarh.
  • Compare the mature Gupta Buddha sculptures of Mathura and Sarnath with earlier Kushan period works.

1. The Gupta Golden Age

The Gupta period (c. 4th to 6th century CE) is universally hailed as the 'Golden Age' of Indian art, science, and literature. Under the patronage of powerful rulers like Chandragupta II and Kumargupta, art transcended its earlier robust and earthy forms to achieve a state of sublime spiritual refinement and classical perfection. This period saw the codification of iconographic rules (Shilpa Shastras), ensuring a standardized, pan-Indian aesthetic. The Gupta style became the definitive model, not just within the Indian subcontinent, but across Southeast Asia.

In sculptural art, the sensual vigor of the Kushan period (Mathura school) was tempered by a profound intellectual and spiritual calm. The heavy, muscular bodies were replaced by slender, idealized forms that seemed to float weightlessly. The drapery, particularly in the Sarnath school, became entirely transparent, clinging to the body without the heavy folds seen in Gandhara or earlier Mathura works, emphasizing the inner spiritual radiance (prana) over physical anatomy.

For the exam, the key differentiator for Gupta art is 'balance'. It is the perfect synthesis of the external physical form and internal spiritual essence. When asked to identify a Gupta period sculpture, look for the serene, downcast eyes, the slight, meditative smile, and the elaborate, uncarved halos (prabhamandala) behind the head, which contrast with the highly decorated halos of the Mathura school.

2. Evolution of the Buddha Image: Sarnath and Mathura

During the Gupta period, the Buddhist centers of Mathura and Sarnath reached their zenith, producing some of the most iconic images of the Buddha in world art. While both schools shared the Gupta emphasis on spiritual serenity, they had distinct regional variations that are frequently tested by the ERB.

The Mathura Buddha of the Gupta period continued to use the characteristic red sandstone. However, compared to the earlier Kushan period, the Gupta-Mathura Buddha exhibits a more slender, relaxed posture. The drapery, while still showing schematic folds (string-like ridges), covers both shoulders. The face is more oval, with half-closed eyes signifying deep meditation. A hallmark of the Mathura Gupta Buddha is the highly ornate halo, intricately carved with floral motifs and concentric circles.

In contrast, the Sarnath School (near Varanasi) used Chunar sandstone and took the Gupta aesthetic to its ultimate conclusion. The most famous example is the Seated Buddha preaching his first sermon (Dharmachakra Pravartana mudra). In Sarnath sculptures, the drapery is completely smooth and transparent, devoid of any folds, clinging to the body like a wet garment. The halo is typically plain, emphasizing the profound stillness of the figure. Understanding this distinction—Mathura (stringed folds, ornate halo) vs. Sarnath (smooth drapery, plain halo)—is crucial for answering specific MCQs.

3. The Dawn of Structural Temples

While the Maurya and Kushan periods were dominated by rock-cut architecture (caves) and stupas, the Gupta period marks the genesis of the free-standing, structural Hindu temple. Early Gupta temples were relatively simple, consisting of a flat-roofed, square sanctum (Garbhagriha) with a shallow pillared porch (Mandapa) in front. Over time, these evolved to include a circumambulatory path (Pradakshina Patha) and a low tower (Shikhara) over the sanctum.

The Dashavatara Temple at Deogarh (Uttar Pradesh) is a quintessential example of late Gupta structural architecture and a highly testable topic. Dedicated to Lord Vishnu, it is one of the earliest Indian temples to feature a rudimentary Shikhara (tower). It is constructed on a raised platform (Jagati) and is renowned for its magnificent sculpted panels depicting scenes from Vaishnava mythology.

The three most famous panels at Deogarh are: 1) Vishnu Anantashayana (Vishnu reclining on the serpent Shesha, symbolizing the preservation of the universe between cosmic cycles), 2) Gajendra Moksha (Vishnu rescuing the elephant king), and 3) Nara-Narayana (the twin sages in deep penance). These panels demonstrate the Gupta mastery of narrative sculpture, where divine figures are rendered with exquisite grace and perfect proportions. Memorizing the location (Deogarh), deity (Vishnu), and specific narrative panels is essential for securing marks in this section.

Worked Example

Q: Which of the following is a key distinguishing feature of a Sarnath School Buddha sculpture from the Gupta period?

Step 1: Identify the period and school: Gupta Period, Sarnath School.

Step 2: Recall the characteristics of Sarnath versus Mathura in the Gupta period. Mathura retains some folds, Sarnath eliminates them entirely.

Answer: Completely smooth and transparent drapery with no visible folds.

🧠 How ERB Asks It

ERB frequently asks to match the temple with its location and deity. You must memorize: Dashavatara Temple -> Deogarh (UP) -> Lord Vishnu. Also, expect questions comparing the 'transparent' drapery of Sarnath with the 'stringed folds' of Mathura.

Quick Recap

  • The Gupta period is the 'Golden Age', characterized by spiritual serenity, balanced proportions, and classical refinement.
  • The Sarnath Buddha is noted for its completely smooth, transparent drapery and plain halo.
  • The Mathura Gupta Buddha retains string-like drapery folds and features a highly ornate halo.
  • The Dashavatara Temple at Deogarh is a classic example of early structural Hindu architecture featuring a rudimentary Shikhara and famous Vishnu relief panels.
