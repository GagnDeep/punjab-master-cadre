Buddhist and Mauryan Art

ਪਾਠ 2: ਬੋਧੀ ਅਤੇ ਮੌਰੀਆ ਕਾਲ ਦੀ ਕਲਾ

Why this matters (ERB 2022)

Questions on Mauryan art frequently test your knowledge of Ashokan pillars, their capitals (like the Lion Capital), and the structural elements of Buddhist Stupas (Sanchi, Bharhut).

ਮੌਰੀਆ ਕਾਲ ਦੇ ਸਵਾਲਾਂ ਵਿੱਚ ਅਸ਼ੋਕ ਦੇ ਸਤੰਭਾਂ (Pillars) ਅਤੇ ਸਾਂਚੀ ਦੇ ਸਤੂਪ ਦੀਆਂ ਬਾਰੀਕੀਆਂ ਬਾਰੇ ਪੁੱਛਿਆ ਜਾਂਦਾ ਹੈ। ਇਹਨਾਂ ਦੇ ਵੱਖ-ਵੱਖ ਹਿੱਸਿਆਂ ਦੇ ਨਾਮ ਯਾਦ ਰੱਖਣਾ ਬਹੁਤ ਜ਼ਰੂਰੀ ਹੈ।

By the end of this lesson, you will:

  • Deconstruct the architectural components of a Buddhist Stupa (Anda, Harmika, Chhatri, Torana).
  • Analyze the significance and symbolism of the Ashokan Pillars and the Lion Capital at Sarnath.
  • Differentiate between the Gandhara and Mathura schools of Buddhist art.
  • Recognize the shift from wood to stone architecture during the Mauryan period.

1. Mauryan Court Art: The Shift to Stone

The Mauryan Empire (c. 322–185 BCE) marks a monumental shift in Indian art history: the transition from wood and brick to durable stone. This period, particularly under the reign of Emperor Ashoka, witnessed the emergence of monumental stone architecture and sculpture, driven largely by imperial patronage and the spread of Buddhism. The use of Chunar sandstone, characterized by a highly polished, mirror-like finish known as the 'Mauryan polish', became a defining hallmark of this era's court art. This polish was so exceptional that subsequent generations often mistook the stone pillars for metal.

The most iconic symbols of Mauryan art are the monolithic Ashokan pillars. Erected across the subcontinent, these pillars served both as imperial edicts and as symbols of the cosmic axis (axis mundi). They were typically composed of a long, tapering monolithic shaft topped by a bell-shaped lotus capital, an abacus, and a crowning animal figure. The most famous of these is the Lion Capital of Sarnath, which was adopted as the National Emblem of India. It features four Asiatic lions standing back-to-back, representing power, courage, pride, and confidence, resting on a circular abacus carved with a galloping horse, a bull, a lion, and an elephant, separated by intervening Dharma Chakras (wheels of law).

When analyzing Mauryan art for the Master Cadre exam, it is crucial to distinguish between court art (initiated by the Emperor) and popular art (created by local artisans). While the highly polished pillars represent court art, the terracotta figurines and large stone statues of Yakshas and Yakshinis (nature spirits), such as the famous Didarganj Yakshini, represent the continuation of popular indigenous art traditions. These figures are characterized by their massive size, frontality, and earthly vitality, contrasting with the refined elegance of the Ashokan capitals.

2. The Stupa Architecture

Simultaneous with the erection of pillars, the Mauryan and subsequent Shunga-Satavahana periods saw the evolution of Buddhist Stupa architecture. A stupa originated as a simple burial mound covering the relics of the Buddha or prominent monks but evolved into a complex architectural and cosmological symbol. Understanding the structural components of a stupa is essential, as terminology-based questions are frequent in the ERB exams.

The core of the stupa is the 'Anda', a massive hemispherical dome symbolizing the dome of heaven or the cosmic egg. Atop the Anda rests the 'Harmika', a square, fence-like enclosure that represents the sacred abode of the gods. From the center of the Harmika rises the 'Yasti' (a central mast representing the cosmic axis), which supports the 'Chhatra' (a series of tiered umbrellas symbolizing royalty and spiritual protection). The entire structure is surrounded by a 'Vedika' (stone railing) and accessed through four intricately carved gateways known as 'Toranas', positioned at the cardinal directions.

The Great Stupa at Sanchi (initially built by Ashoka and later expanded) is the finest surviving example. The Toranas at Sanchi are covered in exquisite narrative relief sculptures depicting Jataka tales (stories of the Buddha's past lives) and scenes from the Buddha's life. Crucially, in early Buddhist art (Hinayana phase), the Buddha was never depicted in human form; instead, his presence was indicated through symbols such as the Bodhi tree, an empty throne, footprints, or a riderless horse. Recognizing this aniconic (symbolic) representation is a key point for examination purposes.

3. Gandhara and Mathura Schools

In the post-Mauryan period, particularly under the Kushan Empire (1st to 3rd century CE), two distinct schools of sculptural art emerged: the Gandhara School in the northwest and the Mathura School in northern India. This era marks the transition from aniconic representations to the creation of the first anthropomorphic (human-form) images of the Buddha, driven by the rise of Mahayana Buddhism. Differentiating between these two schools is a high-yield topic for the Master Cadre exam.

The Gandhara School developed in modern-day Pakistan and Afghanistan. It was heavily influenced by Greco-Roman aesthetics, a legacy of the Indo-Greek kingdoms. Gandharan Buddhas are typically carved from bluish-grey schist stone. They are characterized by realistic anatomical details, wavy hair tied in a topknot (ushnisha), a prominent forehead mark (urna), and heavy, toga-like drapery with deep, naturalistic folds. The facial features often resemble those of the Greek god Apollo, reflecting a syncretic blend of Indian spiritual concepts and Hellenistic artistic techniques.

In contrast, the Mathura School was an indigenous development, utilizing the local spotted red sandstone. The Mathura Buddha is distinctly Indian in character. He is usually depicted with a shaved head (or closely cropped curls), broad shoulders, a smiling countenance, and a fleshy, robust physique reflecting inner yogic vitality (prana). The drapery in Mathura sculptures is typically sheer and clings to the body, revealing the form beneath, unlike the heavy folds of Gandhara. Furthermore, the Mathura school also produced significant sculptures of Jain Tirthankaras and Hindu deities, making it a crucial center for the development of pan-Indian iconography.

Worked Example

Q: Which of the following schools of art was primarily known for using bluish-grey schist stone and exhibiting significant Hellenistic influence in its depiction of the Buddha?

Step 1: Identify the key characteristics: 'bluish-grey schist stone' and 'Hellenistic (Greek) influence'.

Step 2: Differentiate between the indigenous Mathura school (red sandstone, Indian features) and the Greco-Buddhist Gandhara school.

Answer: The Gandhara School.

🧠 How ERB Asks It

ERB tests heavily on architectural terminology. Expect questions asking you to identify the 'Harmika', 'Anda', or 'Torana' of a Stupa. Also, compare-and-contrast questions between Gandhara (grey stone, Greek influence) and Mathura (red sandstone, indigenous style) are guaranteed.

Quick Recap

  • Mauryan court art is defined by monumental stone architecture and the highly polished Ashokan pillars.
  • The Stupa evolved from a burial mound to a complex architectural symbol representing Buddhist cosmology.
  • Early Buddhist art used symbols (aniconic) to represent the Buddha, while later periods introduced human forms.
  • The Gandhara school shows Greco-Roman influence, whereas the Mathura school is entirely indigenous.

ਹੁਣ ਆਪਾਂ ਗੱਲ ਕਰਦੇ ਹਾਂ 'ਸਤੂਪ' (Stupa) ਦੀ। ਸਾਂਚੀ ਦਾ ਸਤੂਪ ਬਹੁਤ ਮਸ਼ਹੂਰ ਹੈ। ਸਤੂਪ ਅਸਲ ਵਿੱਚ ਮਹਾਤਮਾ ਬੁੱਧ ਜਾਂ ਵੱਡੇ ਬੋਧੀ ਭਿਕਸ਼ੂਆਂ ਦੀਆਂ ਨਿਸ਼ਾਨੀਆਂ ਉੱਤੇ ਬਣਾਇਆ ਗਿਆ ਇੱਕ ਗੁੰਬਦ (dome) ਹੁੰਦਾ ਹੈ। ਐਗਜ਼ਾਮੀਨਰ ਸਤੂਪ ਦੇ ਵੱਖ-ਵੱਖ ਹਿੱਸਿਆਂ ਦੇ ਨਾਮ ਪੁੱਛਦਾ ਹੈ। ਜਿਵੇਂ ਕਿ ਮੁੱਖ ਗੁੰਬਦ ਨੂੰ 'ਅੰਡਾ' (Anda) ਕਿਹਾ ਜਾਂਦਾ ਹੈ। ਇਸਦੇ ਉੱਪਰ ਇੱਕ ਚੌਰਸ ਬਕਸੇ ਵਰਗਾ ਢਾਂਚਾ ਹੁੰਦਾ ਹੈ ਜਿਸਨੂੰ 'ਹਰਮਿਕਾ' (Harmika) ਕਹਿੰਦੇ ਹਨ। ਹਰਮਿਕਾ ਨੂੰ ਦੇਵਤਿਆਂ ਦਾ ਘਰ ਮੰਨਿਆ ਜਾਂਦਾ ਹੈ। ਹਰਮਿਕਾ ਦੇ ਵਿਚਕਾਰ ਇੱਕ ਡੰਡਾ ਹੁੰਦਾ ਹੈ ਜਿਸਨੂੰ 'ਯਸਤੀ' (Yasti) ਕਹਿੰਦੇ ਹਨ, ਅਤੇ ਇਸਦੇ ਉੱਪਰ ਛਤਰੀਆਂ (Chhatra) ਲੱਗੀਆਂ ਹੁੰਦੀਆਂ ਹਨ। ਸਤੂਪ ਦੇ ਚਾਰੇ ਪਾਸੇ ਜੋ ਸੁੰਦਰ ਦਰਵਾਜ਼ੇ ਲੱਗੇ ਹੁੰਦੇ ਹਨ, ਉਹਨਾਂ ਨੂੰ 'ਤੋਰਣ' (Torana) ਕਿਹਾ ਜਾਂਦਾ ਹੈ। ਇਹਨਾਂ ਤੋਰਣਾਂ ਉੱਤੇ ਬੁੱਧ ਦੇ ਜੀਵਨ ਦੀਆਂ ਕਹਾਣੀਆਂ (ਜਾਤਕ ਕਥਾਵਾਂ) ਉੱਕਰੀਆਂ ਹੋਈਆਂ ਹਨ। ਤੁਹਾਨੂੰ ਇਹਨਾਂ ਸਾਰੇ ਹਿੱਸਿਆਂ ਦੇ ਨਾਮ ਅਤੇ ਇਹਨਾਂ ਦਾ ਮਤਲਬ ਪਤਾ ਹੋਣਾ ਚਾਹੀਦਾ ਹੈ।

ਇੱਕ ਗੱਲ ਹੋਰ ਧਿਆਨ ਵਿੱਚ ਰੱਖੋ, ਸ਼ੁਰੂਆਤੀ ਬੋਧੀ ਕਲਾ ਵਿੱਚ ਮਹਾਤਮਾ ਬੁੱਧ ਦੀ ਮੂਰਤੀ ਨਹੀਂ ਬਣਾਈ ਜਾਂਦੀ ਸੀ। ਉਹਨਾਂ ਨੂੰ ਪ੍ਰਤੀਕਾਂ (symbols) ਰਾਹੀਂ ਦਿਖਾਇਆ ਜਾਂਦਾ ਸੀ, ਜਿਵੇਂ ਕਿ ਬੋਹੜ ਦਾ ਦਰੱਖਤ (Bodhi tree), ਖਾਲੀ ਕੁਰਸੀ (empty throne), ਜਾਂ ਪੈਰਾਂ ਦੇ ਨਿਸ਼ਾਨ (footprints)। ਬਾਅਦ ਵਿੱਚ, ਜਦੋਂ ਮੂਰਤੀਆਂ ਬਣਨੀਆਂ ਸ਼ੁਰੂ ਹੋਈਆਂ, ਤਾਂ ਦੋ ਮੁੱਖ ਸਕੂਲ ਸਾਹਮਣੇ ਆਏ: ਗੰਧਾਰ ਸਕੂਲ (Gandhara School) ਅਤੇ ਮਥੁਰਾ ਸਕੂਲ (Mathura School)। ਇਹਨਾਂ ਦੋਵਾਂ ਦਾ ਫਰਕ ਸਮਝਣਾ ਬਹੁਤ ਜ਼ਰੂਰੀ ਹੈ, ਕਿਉਂਕਿ ਇੱਥੋਂ ਸਵਾਲ ਪੱਕਾ ਆਉਂਦਾ ਹੈ।
