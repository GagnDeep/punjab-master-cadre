Phonemes, Syllables, Stress & Intonation

Phonemes

  • The smallest meaning-distinguishing unit of sound.
  • Abstract mental representation of a sound.
  • Written within slashes: /p/, /t/, /k/

Minimal Pairs

  • Two words identical in every way except for one sound segment.
  • Used to prove that a sound is a distinct phoneme.
  • Example: pat vs. bat, sip vs. zip.

Allophones

  • Predictable phonetic variants of a phoneme.
  • Do not change the meaning of a word.
  • Example: The aspirated [pʰ] in 'pin' vs. the unaspirated [p] in 'spin'.

The Syllable

  • A unit of pronunciation larger than a phoneme but smaller than a word.
  • Must contain a peak of sonority (usually a vowel).
  • Structure: Onset + Rhyme (Nucleus + Coda).

Syllable Structure

  • Nucleus: Mandatory vowel.
  • Onset: Initial consonants.
  • Coda: Final consonants.
  • Open Syllable: Ends in a vowel (no coda).
  • Closed Syllable: Ends in a consonant.

Word Stress

  • The relative prominence of a syllable within a word.
  • Characteristics: Louder, longer, higher pitch.
  • English is a stress-timed language.

Stress Placement Rules

  • Nouns/Adjectives: Often stress the first syllable (e.g., RE-cord, PRE-sent).
  • Verbs: Often stress the second syllable (e.g., re-CORD, pre-SENT).
  • Prefixes and suffixes can shift stress.

Intonation

  • The variation of pitch while speaking.
  • Often called the 'melody' of speech.
  • Serves grammatical and emotional functions.

Intonation Patterns

  • Falling Intonation: Used for statements, commands, and Wh-questions.
  • Rising Intonation: Used for Yes/No questions and indicating surprise.
  • Fall-Rise: Indicates uncertainty or implication.

Exam Tips

  • Be able to count syllables in a word.
  • Know noun vs. verb stress patterns.
  • Identify minimal pairs from a list of options.
