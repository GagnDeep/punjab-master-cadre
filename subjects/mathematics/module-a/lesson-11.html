Lesson 11: Number Theory (Divisibility & Primes)

ਨੰਬਰ ਥਿਊਰੀ (ਭਾਜਯੋਗਤਾ ਅਤੇ ਅਭਾਜ ਸੰਖਿਆਵਾਂ)

Why this matters (ERB Pattern)

Finding the GCD/HCF using the Euclidean algorithm and solving problems based on the fundamental theorem of arithmetic are common. These require quick mental math.

ਯੂਕਲਿਡਿਅਨ ਐਲਗੋਰਿਦਮ ਦੀ ਵਰਤੋਂ ਕਰਕੇ HCF/LCM ਲੱਭਣ ਦੇ ਸਵਾਲ ਆਮ ਹਨ। ਇਹਨਾਂ ਲਈ ਤੇਜ਼ ਮਾਨਸਿਕ ਗਣਨਾ (mental math) ਦੀ ਲੋੜ ਹੁੰਦੀ ਹੈ।

By the end of this lesson, you will:

  • Apply divisibility rules rapidly.
  • Calculate GCD and LCM using prime factorization and the Euclidean algorithm.
  • Understand the relationship: a × b = GCD(a,b) × LCM(a,b).
  • Identify prime numbers and understand the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic.

Primes, GCD, and LCM

Number Theory deals with the properties of integers. The Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic states that every integer greater than 1 can be represented uniquely as a product of prime numbers. This prime factorization is the key to finding the Greatest Common Divisor (GCD) and Least Common Multiple (LCM). To find the GCD, take the lowest power of all common prime factors. To find the LCM, take the highest power of all prime factors present. A fundamental property linking two numbers is that their product equals the product of their GCD and LCM: a × b = GCD(a, b) × LCM(a, b). The Euclidean Algorithm is an efficient method for computing the GCD of two larger numbers by repeatedly dividing and finding remainders until the remainder is zero.

Divisibility rules are your best friends for speed. A number is divisible by 3 if the sum of its digits is divisible by 3; divisible by 4 if the last two digits form a number divisible by 4; and divisible by 9 if the sum of its digits is divisible by 9. The rule for 11 is particularly tested: a number is divisible by 11 if the difference between the sum of the digits in odd positions and the sum of the digits in even positions is either 0 or a multiple of 11. Knowing these rules allows you to quickly break down large numbers into their prime factors without tedious long division.

When dealing with fractions, it is vital to know that two numbers are considered 'co-prime' or 'relatively prime' if their GCD is exactly 1. They do not have to be prime numbers themselves (e.g., 8 and 15 are co-prime). If two numbers are co-prime, their LCM is simply their product. Exam questions often exploit this by asking for the LCM of two consecutive integers. Since consecutive integers are always co-prime, their LCM is invariably their product. Recognizing this instantly saves calculation time.

Common Trap

Applying the formula a × b = GCD × LCM to three or more numbers. This formula is STRICTLY for two numbers only. For three numbers, a × b × c ≠ GCD(a,b,c) × LCM(a,b,c).

Worked Example

Q: The LCM of two numbers is 120 and their GCD is 10. If one of the numbers is 30, what is the other number?

Step 1: Recall the formula: Number1 × Number2 = GCD × LCM.

Step 2: Substitute the known values: 30 × Number2 = 10 × 120.

Step 3: Simplify the right side: 30 × Number2 = 1200.

Step 4: Solve for Number2: Number2 = 1200 / 30 = 40.

Answer: The other number is 40.

🧠 How ERB Asks It

Questions involving finding the number of trailing zeros in a factorial (like 100!) are essentially prime factorization questions. You just need to count the number of 5s in the prime factorization, as there are always more 2s than 5s.

Quick Recap

  • Every integer has a unique prime factorization.
  • GCD = lowest powers of common primes.
  • LCM = highest powers of all primes.
  • a × b = GCD(a, b) × LCM(a, b) (for TWO numbers only).
  • Consecutive integers are always co-prime.
